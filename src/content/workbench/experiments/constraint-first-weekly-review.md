<p class="standfirst">Most weekly reviews begin with inventory. This one began with a single question: what will constrain progress next week?</p>

## Hypothesis

Choosing the limiting constraint before reviewing projects will reduce low-value planning and produce fewer, more consequential commitments.

## Method

For four weekly reviews, I used the following sequence:

1. Write down the most important outcome for the coming week.
2. Identify the one constraint most likely to prevent it.
3. Choose an action that weakens that constraint.
4. Review the remaining projects only for genuine deadlines or dependencies.

I compared the resulting plan with the previous four weeks, looking at the number of commitments, unfinished work and midweek reprioritisation.

## What happened

The plans became shorter: typically three meaningful commitments rather than eight to twelve plausible tasks. More importantly, work that depended on somebody else was surfaced earlier.

The method was less effective during fragmented weeks dominated by appointments. In those cases, available attention—not a project dependency—was the real constraint.

## Conclusion

The hypothesis mostly held. A constraint-first review is now the default, but the constraint may be capacity, coordination or uncertainty rather than a bottleneck inside a project.

## Keep

Continue the experiment as a working practice and revisit it after six weeks.
