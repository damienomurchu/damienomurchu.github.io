## Still in incubation

I use AI a lot. Enough that, from the outside, it might seem strange that I have written almost nothing substantial about it.

That is deliberate.

I do not think I have a mature thesis on AI yet. I have observations, working assumptions, and patterns I keep noticing, but I am still experimenting with it and trying to understand where it actually fits into the way I work.

For now, AI is still in incubation.

That is not unusual for me. There are plenty of subjects I care about that I do not immediately feel the need to write about. I tend to sit with them first. I want to use them, test them, change my mind about them, and see which conclusions survive contact with reality.

Eventually, some of those thoughts become strong enough to write down. Others disappear.

AI is currently somewhere in that process.

## I have observations, not a thesis

There are already a few things I feel reasonably confident about.

AI is useful to me as an accelerator. It shortens the distance between having an idea and having something concrete enough to inspect. That might be a rough implementation, an architecture sketch, a comparison between approaches, an experiment, a first draft, or simply a better articulation of the problem I am trying to solve.

More interestingly, it is making technical experimentation cheaper.

There are plenty of things I would previously have looked at and thought, *that could be interesting*, before deciding that learning enough about the subject to test the idea was not worth the time. Now, I can often get far enough into an unfamiliar area quickly enough to decide whether there is actually something worth pursuing.

That changes the economics of experimentation.

When the cost of trying something falls, the set of things worth trying expands. Some experiments go nowhere, but that is not a failure of the model. The point is that more ideas become cheap enough to test before deciding whether they deserve further investment.

I increasingly think this is one of the more significant effects AI is having on the way I work.

It has also become a useful thinking surface.

I regularly use it to pressure-test ideas, expose assumptions, compare alternatives, or take something that exists vaguely in my head and make it explicit enough to examine properly. Sometimes I ask it to attack an idea. Sometimes I explain something badly and see whether the response exposes what I have failed to articulate. Sometimes the useful outcome is discovering that the thing I thought was interesting is not actually the interesting part at all.

Calling that productivity does not quite capture it.

It is closer to externalising part of the thinking process.

## Cheap generation changes what becomes expensive

One pattern I keep returning to is that AI seems to be reducing the cost of generation much faster than it reduces the cost of judgement.

Code can be generated quickly. So can documentation, plans, diagrams, explanations, alternatives, and implementations. Producing something that looks plausible is becoming increasingly cheap.

But someone still has to decide whether it is any good.

Someone still needs to understand the constraints, notice when an answer is technically correct but operationally poor, recognise when a locally elegant solution creates problems elsewhere in the system, and decide whether the problem was worth solving in the first place.

If producing candidate solutions becomes cheap, then selection becomes more important. Verification becomes more important. Taste becomes more important. Understanding what matters becomes more important.

That feels significant, although I am not yet sure exactly where it leads.

I suspect this is one of the areas where my eventual position on AI will develop.

## I am more interested in behaviour than capability

A lot of discussion around AI focuses on what the models can do.

I find myself increasingly interested in a slightly different question: what does the existence of these capabilities cause us to do differently?

If experimentation becomes cheaper, do engineers explore more alternatives before committing to one? If unfamiliar domains become easier to enter, do specialists become broader? If generating an implementation gets easier, does defining the problem become more valuable? If ten plausible approaches can be produced almost immediately, does choosing between them become the actual work?

And if software itself becomes cheaper to produce, what happens to the cost of owning, securing, operating, understanding, and eventually replacing all of that additional software?

Those questions interest me considerably more than model benchmarks.

They are also questions I do not think I can answer confidently yet.

## I do not want to manufacture a position

There is a strong incentive to have an opinion about AI, preferably a definitive one.

AI will replace software engineers. AI will not replace software engineers. Programming is finished. Programming has never been more important. Everything is about to change. Nothing fundamental is changing.

There is no shortage of certainty available.

I do not feel much urgency to add another confident prediction to the pile.

I would rather let a position emerge from repeated use. That takes longer, but it also gives me a better chance of separating what is genuinely changing from what merely feels important because the technology is new.

I have changed my mind about enough technologies over the years to know that first impressions are not particularly valuable. The more interesting conclusions tend to appear after the novelty wears off and you have lived with something for a while.

AI has not reached that point for me yet.

## Some ideas need to be used before they can be understood

Perhaps that is the more general point.

Not every subject needs to become content immediately.

Some ideas benefit from being allowed to remain unresolved for a while. You can collect evidence without defending a position. You can experiment without needing every experiment to support a narrative. You can notice contradictions without immediately trying to resolve them.

Eventually, patterns start to emerge. Some observations keep surviving. Others turn out to have been artefacts of a particular tool, model, workflow, or moment in time.

That process is valuable.

Right now, I have observations about AI. I do not yet have a thesis about AI, and I am increasingly comfortable with the difference.

What I do suspect is that my eventual position will be less about artificial intelligence itself and more about what happens when the cost of experimentation, iteration, and certain kinds of cognitive work falls dramatically.

That seems like the more interesting question.

For now, I am still gathering evidence.
